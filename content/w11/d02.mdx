Two questions that sound like one.

**Severity: how much damage does this do?**
**Priority: how soon must it be fixed?**

They are independent, and merging them is the mistake almost every beginner makes.

<Video id="severity-priority" />

## Severity

A property of the defect itself. Roughly:

| | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **Critical** | Data loss or corruption, money wrong, a security hole, or the product unusable |
| **High** | A major feature broken with no reasonable workaround |
| **Medium** | Something broken with a workaround, or affecting a subset |
| **Low** | Cosmetic, or a minor inconvenience |

Severity is largely objective. Two testers looking at the same defect should mostly agree.

## Priority

A business decision about ordering. Who is affected, how visible is it, what else is competing, is there a deadline, is there a workaround for now.

Priority is **not** yours alone to set. You propose it; a product owner may change it, and that is the system working.

## The four corners

<SeverityGrid />

Place all six, then check. The two awkward corners are the real test.

**High severity, low priority.** A crash in an admin bulk export when the range exceeds five years. A crash, so severity is high. Only staff hit it, only on an unusual input, and there is a workaround — export in two halves. Genuinely bad, genuinely can wait.

**Low severity, high priority.** The wrong logo on the checkout page. Nothing breaks, no data is wrong, severity is low. Every paying customer sees it and it looks like a scam site, so it is fixed today.

<Callout kind="key">
**Judge by damage, not by how loud the bug is.**

The page that loads scrolled past its own top is obvious and harmless. The endpoint returning every customer's email is invisible and critical.

New testers systematically over-rate what they can see and under-rate what they had to dig for. Correcting that bias is most of what this lesson is for.
</Callout>

## Why both, and not one number

A single "importance" field collapses two different conversations.

With both, you can say: *"this is critical severity but low priority because only two internal users are affected and there is a workaround"* — and everyone understands the trade. With one number you argue about the number instead of about the trade.

When your two differ sharply, **say why in the report**. That sentence is often the most useful thing in it.

## Never cry wolf

<Callout kind="watch">
If everything you file is Critical, nothing is.

The word has to be earned, because a true Critical should get dropped-everything attention — and it only gets that if people trust your labels. A tester who inflates severity is ignored exactly when it matters most.

The reverse is also a failure, and quieter. Under-rating a data leak because it is invisible on screen means it sits in a backlog for a month.
</Callout>

## Mapping to support priorities

If your team also runs P1 to P4 for support tickets, the two systems usually line up like this:

| Bug | Support | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical + highest | **P1** | Money or data wrong, or many users down |
| High + high | **P2** | Blocked with no workaround |
| Medium | **P3** | Broken, workaround exists |
| Low | **P4** | Cosmetic or a question |

Phase 4 uses the P-scale properly. The judgement is the same one.
