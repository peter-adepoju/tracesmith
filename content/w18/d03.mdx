The hardest messages in support are the ones with no good news in them. Handled well, they build more trust than an easy fix does — because anyone can deliver good news.

## Say no early

A soft no is a slow yes, and a slow yes is worse than a fast no. If the answer is no, say it in the first paragraph.

> We will not be able to add this to the export. I know that is not what you were hoping for, so let me explain where that leaves us.

Then the explanation. Then what you *can* do.

The shape is: **no, why, what instead.** Never bury the no in paragraph four — the customer is scanning for it and feels manipulated when they find it there.

## Say what you can do

Almost never nothing:

> We cannot add a custom field to the export, which is a limitation of how the report is generated rather than a decision about your request.
>
> What I can do: the API returns the field you need, so if your team can call `GET /transactions` the data is available today — I can send an example. I have also logged the request with the product team; I cannot promise a date, and I will tell you if it is picked up.

Three things: the no, an alternative that works now, and an honest statement about the future with no fake date.

<Callout kind="key">
**Never say "I will chase it up" unless you have a specific action and a time.**

It is the most common empty promise in support. The customer hears *something is happening*; nothing is.

Either: *"I will ask the payments team today and come back to you by 16:00"* — or be honest that there is nothing to chase: *"This is in the product backlog and I have no visibility of when it will be picked up. I will tell you if that changes."*
</Callout>

## Promise a time and keep it

Two rules:

**Promise long, deliver early.** If you think two hours, say four. Being early is free; being late costs the trust you were buying.

**Keep it even when you have nothing.** This is the one people get wrong.

## The no-news update

A customer waiting without news assumes they have been forgotten, and they are usually right.

Send the update anyway:

> Quick update as promised: I do not have an answer yet. The payments team has confirmed the payout left us on 6 November with the bank reference I sent you, and they are tracing it with the bank now. The bank's response time on a trace is typically two working days.
>
> Nothing more for me to tell you today. My next update will be Thursday by midday, sooner if the bank comes back before then.

That message contains no progress and does several things: it proves you remembered, it shows what is happening, it keeps the commitment, and it resets the next one.

<Callout kind="job">
A customer who gets a "no news yet" message on time almost never complains about the delay. A customer who gets silence complains about the delay *and* about being ignored, and the second complaint is the one that escalates to your manager.

The no-news update is five minutes and it is the single cheapest piece of goodwill in support.
</Callout>

## Delivering a real failure

When something has genuinely gone wrong on your side:

1. **Say what happened**, plainly, without hiding behind the passive voice. Not "an error occurred" — "we double-charged you".
2. **Say what it means for them**, specifically and in money where money is involved.
3. **Say what you have done**, already, before telling them.
4. **Say what happens next**, with a time.
5. **Apologise once**, properly, and move on.

> You were charged twice for order 8841 — NGN 48,500 on each. That was our error, not your bank's.
>
> I have refunded the duplicate charge this morning; the reference is `REF-2291`. Your bank will typically post it within two working days, so by Thursday. If it has not appeared by then, reply here and I will chase it with them directly.
>
> I am sorry. You should not have to check your statement because of something we did.

No "unfortunately", no "any inconvenience", no explanation of the technical cause the customer did not ask for. The fix came before the apology, which is what makes the apology credible.

## When you were wrong

You will tell a customer something that turns out to be untrue. Correct it fast, yourself, before they find out:

> I need to correct something I told you yesterday. I said the refund had been issued; it had been approved but not actually released, and it went out this morning. That is my mistake and I am sorry — here is the reference.

Say what you said, say what is true, say it was yours. No explanation of how the mistake happened; it is not interesting to them, and it reads as excuse-making.
