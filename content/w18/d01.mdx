Most of your influence travels through your writing. The same facts, written two ways, produce a calm customer and a fast fix — or a complaint and three follow-up questions.

This week is the writing.

## The four-part reply

Every customer message, however short:

1. **Acknowledge** — you have read it and you understand what is at stake
2. **Own** — someone specific is handling it, and that someone is you
3. **Next step** — what is actually happening now
4. **Time** — when they will hear from you

Three sentences can carry all four:

> Thanks for flagging this — I can see the payment of NGN 48,500 has not reached your account and I understand that is blocking your reconciliation. I am tracing it with our payments team now. I will come back to you by 12:00 today with either the payment or a clear explanation of where it is.

Acknowledge, own, next step, time. Nothing guessed, nothing promised that is not within your control.

<Callout kind="key">
**The time commitment is the part that calms people, and the part most often left out.**

Most anger in support is not about the problem. It is about not knowing — whether anyone is looking, whether it will be today or next week, whether they need to chase.

A time removes all three questions at once. It costs nothing, and it is the single highest-value sentence in support writing.
</Callout>

## No jargon

The customer does not know what a webhook is, and does not need to.

| Jargon | Plain |
| --- | --- |
| The webhook callback failed to deliver | Our system did not manage to tell the shop that your payment went through |
| The transaction is in a pending settlement state | The money has left your card and is on its way to the merchant; it usually lands the next working day |
| It returned a 502 from upstream | The other system was not responding when we tried |
| We will need to escalate to engineering | I am passing this to the team who can fix it, and staying on it myself |

The test: **would someone outside tech understand this on one reading?**

This is not dumbing down. Writing plainly is harder than writing technically, and it is what the job is.

## Calming a situation in three sentences

When someone is angry, the instinct is to explain. Explanation reads as defence, and defence escalates.

1. **Agree with what is true.** "You are right, two days without a reply is not acceptable."
2. **State what is happening now.** "I have your case and I am looking at it myself."
3. **Give a time.** "You will hear from me by 14:00 today whether or not I have the full answer."

No excuses, no "unfortunately", no explaining the queue. Nobody has ever been calmed by hearing how busy you are.

## Words that cost you

**"Unfortunately"** — a signal to brace. Say the thing instead.

**"As I mentioned"** / "as per my last email" — reads as *you did not read it*, even when true.

**"Simply"** / "just" — "just click Settings" implies it was obvious and they are slow. Delete both words, always.

**"We apologise for any inconvenience caused"** — a formula that says nobody read this. "I am sorry — this should not have happened to you" is a sentence a person wrote.

**"The system is down"** when you mean one feature is degraded. Precision is kinder, because it tells them what still works.

<Callout kind="watch">
**Never promise what you do not control.**

"The refund will be in your account within 24 hours" depends on their bank. "We are releasing a fix tomorrow" depends on engineering.

Promise your own actions: *"I will release the refund today and send you the reference. Their bank usually posts within two working days — if it has not arrived by Thursday, reply here and I will chase it."*

That commits to what you can do, gives a realistic expectation for what you cannot, and gives them a specific next move rather than an open-ended wait.
</Callout>

## Apologising properly

Apologise for what happened, not with a formula, and only once.

- **Weak:** "We apologise for any inconvenience this may have caused."
- **Strong:** "I am sorry — you should not have had to chase this twice."

Then stop. Repeated apology shifts the conversation to managing your discomfort, and the customer wants their problem solved, not your feelings tended.
