Almost every long support conversation was made long by a first reply that did not ask for enough.

## The four things, every time

1. **The exact error** — the wording, or a screenshot. "An error" is not an error.
2. **The time** — with a timezone. "This morning" is not a time.
3. **The account or reference** — an account id, a transaction reference, an order number.
4. **What they were doing** — the exact step.

With those four you can usually find the event in under a minute. Without them you are guessing, and guessing is how a ticket takes four days instead of four minutes.

<Callout kind="key">
**Ask for one specific instance.**

"Can you give me the reference for one transaction that failed?" is the highest-value question in support. One real example beats any amount of description, because you can look it up rather than imagine it.
</Callout>

## Gather it in one message

The amateur version is four replies over two days, one question each. Ask once, warmly, and make it easy:

> Thanks for letting us know, and sorry for the trouble. So I can find exactly what happened rather than guess, could you send me:
>
> - the reference of one payment that failed (it starts with `PAY-`)
> - roughly what time you tried, and
> - the exact wording of the message you saw — a screenshot is perfect
>
> As soon as I have those I will look it up and come back to you today.

Three asks, a reason for asking, and a commitment. Four or five asks start to read as an obstacle course; two is better still if you can get away with it.

## The guessing trap

The temptation is to answer immediately from the description, because you have seen something like it before.

- "Your card was probably declined" — when it succeeded and the webhook failed
- "That is a known issue" — when it is a different issue with the same symptom
- "Try clearing your cache" — the reflex that means *I have not looked*

<Callout kind="watch">
Every guess has the same shape: it buys ten quiet minutes and costs a second conversation in which you are now the person who was wrong.

The customer is also not neutral about this. They can tell the difference between "I checked and here is what I found" and "try turning it off and on again", and the second one spends trust you will need later.
</Callout>

## Pattern-matching, used properly

Recognising a familiar shape is a genuine skill. The rule is that it tells you **where to look first**, never what to say.

> This looks like the webhook-delay pattern we saw last week. *So I will check the delivery log for this reference* — not: *so I will tell them it is a webhook delay*.

## When they cannot give you what you asked for

Some customers will not have a reference, or will not know the time.

- Narrow it: "Was it before or after lunch?"
- Use what they do have: their email, phone number or account name is usually enough to find the account, then the recent activity.
- Look it up yourself where you can. Asking for something you could find in your own admin tool is lazy and reads that way.

**Never make the customer do your work.** Ask for what only they have — what they saw, what they did, when. Find the rest yourself.
