Two words that sound alike, mean different things, and map onto two different status codes. Getting them straight is the whole of today, and it will settle a surprising number of tickets.

<Video id="authn-vs-authz" />

## The distinction

**Authentication** — *who are you?* Proving identity.

**Authorization** — *what are you allowed to do?* Checking permission.

They happen in that order, and they are separate decisions. A system can know exactly who you are and still refuse you.

| | Question | Failure | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Authentication** | Who are you? | We do not know you | **401** |
| **Authorization** | May you do this? | We know you, and no | **403** |

<Callout kind="key">
**401 is identity. 403 is permission.**

It is worth noticing that 401 is named "Unauthorized", which is exactly the wrong word — it is about *authentication*. That misnaming is baked into the standard and it confuses people permanently. Remember the meaning, not the name.
</Callout>

## Why it matters in a ticket

The two have completely different fixes, and different owners.

**401** — the credential is missing, mistyped, expired, or for the wrong environment. Usually the caller can fix it themselves, and often in seconds.

**403** — the credential is fine. This account is not permitted. Nobody fixes that by retrying; it needs a permission change, a different key, or an honest "you cannot do this".

Telling a merchant to "check your API key" when they are getting a 403 wastes everyone's afternoon. Their key is perfect. It is simply not allowed to do what they are asking.

## The one that bit you

Last week you hit this exactly. Sign-in returned **"Invalid login credentials"**, which reads like a wrong password — authentication failing on the password itself.

The real cause was an unconfirmed account. Supabase returns the same message for both, deliberately, so that nobody can probe which email addresses exist by watching the error change.

<Callout kind="watch">
That is a real and deliberate pattern: **a good login endpoint gives the same answer for "no such user" and "wrong password"**. If it distinguished them, anyone could discover which addresses have accounts by trying them.

So an unhelpful login error is sometimes a security feature. The cost is exactly what you experienced — it is harder to diagnose, including for the people who built it.
</Callout>

## Feel the difference

<RequestInspector preset="No key at all" />

Three requests, three outcomes. Send each and read the body as well as the code.

1. **No key at all** → 401. No identity offered.
2. **Right call, wrong key** (the public key on a customers endpoint) → 403. Identity accepted, permission refused.
3. Set the Authorization to `pk_test_nonsense` → 401. A credential was offered and not recognised.

Numbers 1 and 3 are both 401 for different reasons — absent versus unrecognised — and the body distinguishes them. Number 2 is the one where the key is entirely valid.
