Work arrives from everywhere at once, in no order, and some of it is the same problem wearing different clothes. Today: how to receive it without losing any of it.

## The channels

**Email** — slow, detailed, easy to lose in a shared inbox nobody owns.

**Live chat** — fast, expects an answer now, and the context vanishes when the window closes unless something captures it.

**Phone** — richest and most demanding. Nothing is recorded unless you record it, which means it is the channel where work most often disappears.

**Social media** — public, so it carries reputational weight out of proportion to its severity. Move it to a private channel quickly and politely.

**In-app or portal** — the best of them. Arrives with the account, the device and often the failing transaction already attached.

**Internal** — Slack from sales, a nudge in a corridor, an operations request. The channel most likely to bypass your system entirely.

<Callout kind="key">
**Every channel must land in one record.**

A ticket for a phone call. A ticket for the Slack message from sales. A ticket for the conversation you had in the corridor.

Two reasons, and the second is the one people miss:

1. Work that is not written down is work that gets dropped, and you cannot see your own queue.
2. **Patterns only exist in the aggregate.** Four people mentioning a slow dashboard across four channels is an incident. Four separate conversations in four places is four shrugs.
</Callout>

## Why first-come-first-served fails

It is fair, it is simple, and it is wrong.

At 08:02 a merchant reports that all card payments are declining. At 08:05 someone asks how to change their email address. At 08:21 an enterprise customer reports a missing settlement of four million naira.

In arrival order you answer the email question second, while money is stopped for two businesses.

**Order by consequence, not by arrival.** The queue is not a line at a counter; it is a set of problems with different costs per hour of delay.

## Urgency and impact

Two axes, like severity and priority in week 11 — and for the same reason, because collapsing them loses the trade-off.

**Impact** — how many people, and how badly. One user inconvenienced, or every merchant unable to take money.

**Urgency** — how fast the damage grows. A missing settlement gets worse hourly as a business cannot pay suppliers. A blurry logo is equally blurry next week.

| | Low impact | High impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High urgency** | P3 — one person blocked now | **P1** — many people, money stopped |
| **Low urgency** | P4 — cosmetic | P2 — wrong for many, not yet costly |

Tomorrow's lesson and the day after put numbers and deadlines on this.

## Triage is its own pass

Do not triage by working. Triage the **whole queue first**, quickly, then work it in order.

Fifteen tickets take about four minutes to triage — enough to read each one and assign a priority, not to solve any. That four minutes is what stops you spending the morning on the first thing you opened.

<Callout kind="job">
Re-triage after any new information. A P3 at nine can be a P1 at ten because two more reports of the same symptom arrived.

Conversely, a P1 drops once you know it is one customer with a local network problem. Priority is a current judgement, not a label applied once.
</Callout>

## Spotting the incident early

The single most valuable triage habit: **notice when two tickets are one problem.**

Signals:
- The same symptom from different customers
- Start times within minutes of each other
- A sudden spike in volume on any one topic
- A symptom that does not match the usual customer confusion

Two reports of card declines starting at 07:50 is not two tickets. It is an incident, and the correct action is to raise it as one thing and tell both customers — not to debug two cases in parallel.

And once you have seen two, **go looking for the third** rather than waiting for it to arrive.

## One record per customer

Beyond one record per issue: all of a customer's history in one place.

Because the fourth ticket from the same merchant this month is a different conversation from the first. Without history you cannot see that, you ask them to re-explain, and they tell you — correctly — that they have explained this three times already.
