Accessibility is not a specialism you need a certificate for. There are four checks you can run on any screen, today, in about ten minutes, and between them they find most of what is wrong.

## Check 1 — the keyboard pass

Put the mouse away. Tab through the whole screen.

You are watching for four things:

- **Can you reach everything?** Every control that does something.
- **Can you see where you are?** A visible focus indicator, always.
- **Does the order make sense?** Roughly the order things appear on screen.
- **Can you get out?** No trap — in particular, can you close a modal with Escape?

<KeyboardPass />

Tab through it with the faults on, then turn them off one at a time and Tab again. Three faults, three findings, and all three are ones you will meet in real products.

<Callout kind="key">
**The keyboard pass costs two minutes per screen and finds more than any automated scanner.**

A scanner can tell you an image has no `alt`. It cannot tell you the focus order jumps from the header to the footer and back, because nothing in the markup is technically wrong — it just makes no sense to use.

This is also not only about disability. Keyboard users include anyone with a broken trackpad, anyone fast, and every power user you have.
</Callout>

## Check 2 — images and buttons

**Every image needs an `alt`.** Meaningful images get a description of what they convey; decorative ones get `alt=""`, which tells a screen reader to skip them. A missing attribute is not the same as an empty one, and an empty one is often correct.

Bad alt text is its own defect: `alt="image"`, `alt="photo123.jpg"`, or a product photo described as `alt="product"`.

**Every icon-only button needs a name.** The commonest finding in any audit:

<AccessibleName />

Turn everything off. That is a button a screen reader announces as "button" — the user is told there is something clickable and nothing about what it does.

## Check 3 — contrast

Text must stand clear of its background. The thresholds:

- **4.5:1** for normal text
- **3:1** for large text (18pt and up, or 14pt bold)

Check it in DevTools: inspect the element, click the colour swatch in the Styles panel, and the contrast ratio is shown with a tick or a cross against both thresholds.

The usual offenders are light grey placeholder text, disabled states, and white text on a brand colour that was chosen for a logo rather than for reading.

## Check 4 — zoom and reflow

Set the browser to **200%** zoom.

Does the content reflow into one column and stay usable, or does it overlap, clip, or force sideways scrolling? Text being cut off at 200% is a finding, and it affects far more people than most teams assume.

## Writing an accessibility finding

Same report as any bug, with three additions:

- **Who it affects** — "keyboard users", "screen reader users", "anyone with low vision"
- **The barrier** — reached, or blocked entirely
- **The guideline**, where you know it — WCAG 2.1 has numbered criteria, and citing one moves the conversation from opinion to standard

> **The Filter control cannot be reached by keyboard (product list).** It is a `<div>` with a click handler, so it takes no focus and no key activates it. Keyboard and screen reader users cannot filter at all — the feature does not exist for them. WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A). Fix: use a `<button>`.

Severity: a control that cannot be reached is **high**, because for that user the feature is absent, not awkward.

<Callout kind="job">
In Nigeria, the EU and the US, accessibility is increasingly a legal requirement rather than a nice-to-have, and for public-sector or enterprise buyers it is often a procurement condition.

That is worth knowing because it changes how a finding is received. "This is a WCAG Level A failure" lands differently from "this would be nice to fix".
</Callout>
