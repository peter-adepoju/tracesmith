The same page, the same build, two browsers, two different results. This is normal, and knowing *why* is what lets you write a report a developer can act on.

## Why browsers differ

Three reasons, and they are not the same kind of problem:

**Engines.** Chrome and Edge use Blink, Firefox uses Gecko, Safari uses WebKit. Three separate implementations of a very large specification, each with its own bugs and its own pace.

**Support.** A CSS or JavaScript feature arrives in one engine before the others. Safari in particular is the one that lags, and because iOS requires WebKit, *every* browser on an iPhone is Safari underneath — including Chrome for iOS. A bug "only in Chrome on iPhone" is a Safari bug.

**Defaults.** Form controls, date pickers, fonts and scrollbars are styled by the browser itself. A date input is three dropdowns in one and a text field in another.

<Callout kind="key">
**"It works on my machine" is not a defence, and neither is "it is broken in Safari".**

The useful report names the engine, the version, the platform, and says whether it reproduces elsewhere. That is what turns a vague complaint into a ticket someone can pick up.
</Callout>

## Which browsers do you owe?

Not all of them. Your analytics decide it: take the browsers covering roughly 95% of your actual users, plus anything the business has committed to.

A realistic set for most products:

- Chrome, current, desktop
- Safari, current, desktop and iOS
- Firefox, current, desktop
- Edge, current, desktop
- Chrome, current, Android

Write it down and agree it. An undocumented support matrix means every browser bug becomes an argument about whether it counts.

## Responsive, which is a different question

Cross-browser is *which engine*. Responsive is *which width*. They fail differently and you test them separately.

Three widths, at minimum:

- **375px** — a phone. The hardest one, and the most used.
- **768px** — a tablet, and usually the most neglected.
- **1280px** — a desktop.

Test *either side of each breakpoint*, not just at it. The bug lives at 767px or 769px far more often than at exactly 768.

<ViewportSlider />

Drag slowly through the marks. The jumps are the breakpoints, and finding them is the first thing to do on any new feature, because you cannot test either side of a line you have not located.

<Callout kind="watch">
**Resizing a desktop browser is not the same as a phone.** It gets you the layout, and that is genuinely most of it — but it does not get you touch targets, the on-screen keyboard covering the field you are typing into, momentum scrolling, or the address bar resizing the viewport mid-scroll.

Do the width pass in DevTools. Then do a real pass on a real phone before you call it tested.
</Callout>

## Reporting a browser-specific bug

Four things the report must carry:

1. **Where it fails** — browser, version, platform.
2. **Where it does not** — "Chrome 131 and Firefox 133 on the same build are correct."
3. **The engine**, if you know it — "Safari 17.6, so WebKit; likely affects all iOS browsers."
4. Whether it is **layout, behaviour or a console error** — they go to different fixes.

Point 2 does most of the work. A bug that fails everywhere is a product bug; one that fails in a single engine is a compatibility bug, and the fix is a different conversation.
