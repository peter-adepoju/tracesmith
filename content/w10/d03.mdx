Steps are the part people write fastest and should write slowest. A vague step is where "works for me" is born.

## One action per step

```
Bad
1. Log in and go to the cart and apply a discount and check out

Good
1. Sign in as the test merchant
2. Open the cart
3. Enter SAVE10 in the discount field and click Apply
4. Click Checkout
```

Numbering forces this. If a step contains "and", it is usually two steps — and when a case fails, "it failed at step 3" is useful while "it failed at step 1" is not.

## Say exactly what, not approximately where

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| Enter some details | Enter `ada@example.com` in the Email field |
| Add an item | Add **Harmattan Field Jacket** to the cart |
| Use a test card | Enter the Interswitch test card `5060 6666 6666 6666 666` |
| Click the button | Click **Place order** |
| Wait a bit | Wait for the confirmation page to load |

Name the **label**, not the position or the colour. `Click the blue button top right` breaks the first time a designer moves it; `Click Place order` survives.

<Callout kind="watch">
"Wait a bit" deserves its own warning. Timing is where flaky tests come from — in manual cases and far more so in automation.

**Wait for a condition, not a duration.** "Wait until the confirmation page shows the order reference" is checkable. "Wait 3 seconds" passes on a fast day and fails on a slow one, and you will spend a morning on it in week 15.
</Callout>

## Include the values

A step that says "enter a quantity" leaves the tester choosing, and two testers will choose differently. If the quantity matters — and in a boundary case it is the whole point — state it.

Where the exact value genuinely does not matter, say so: *"enter any valid email"*. That is a deliberate instruction, not an omission.

## Keep the goal visible

Steps describe *how*. The title and expected result describe *what for*. When someone can follow all four steps without knowing what is being checked, they will not notice the thing that went wrong beside the thing they were watching.

A good case reads as: **this is what we are checking → here is how → here is what should happen.**

## How many steps

Three to eight is the usual range.

Fewer than three and the case is probably missing its setup or its verification. More than eight and it is probably two cases, or it has setup in it that belongs in preconditions.

If a case has fifteen steps and the first ten are getting into position, move those ten into preconditions and do them through the API. Yesterday's lesson.
