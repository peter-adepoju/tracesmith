The last skill, and the one most people get backwards: making yourself unnecessary to the thing you built.

## The instinct is wrong

Being the only person who understands something feels like security. It is the opposite.

**You cannot be moved.** Not to the interesting project, not to the promotion, because you are load-bearing where you are.

**You are interrupted constantly.** Every question comes to you, forever, including on holiday.

**Your work cannot grow.** Nobody will let a critical process depend on one person who might leave, so it stays small or gets rebuilt by someone else.

**It is fragile, and everyone knows it.** A system with one person who understands it is a risk on somebody's register, and risks get managed — sometimes by replacing them.

<Callout kind="key">
**Being replaceable on each thing is what makes you promotable across things.**

The person who can hand over cleanly is the person who gets given the next project. The person who cannot is the person still running the last one in three years.

This is not a moral point. It is how the incentives actually work, and it is worth understanding early.
</Callout>

## The walkthrough where they drive

Not a demo. A session where **they** hold the keyboard and you sit beside them.

Give them real tasks, in order:

1. Find the workflow and open the last run
2. Tell me whether it succeeded, and how you know
3. Find yesterday's output
4. Show me where the credential lives
5. Here is an alert — what would you do first?
6. Re-run it, safely. Tell me first why that is safe
7. Make a small change, and tell me how you would check you had not broken anything

Your job is to be quiet. Let them look, let them be stuck, let them find it.

Every time you reach for the keyboard, you have found a documentation gap and then covered it up.

## Write it down as you go

Keep the page open during the session. When they ask something, **write the answer into the document rather than saying it.** Then let them read it.

It feels slow and it is the entire point — the session is a test of the documentation, not of them, and answering out loud leaves the gap in place for the next person.

By the end you have a page that has been proven against a real reader.

## What ownership actually transfers

Handing over is not showing someone how it works. It is transferring four things:

**Responsibility.** They are the owner now. The alerts go to them, their name is in the documentation, and this is written down somewhere other than a conversation.

**Authority.** They can change it without asking you. If every change still needs your approval, nothing was transferred.

**Context.** Not only how it works, but why it is the way it is — the decisions, the constraints, the thing you were least sure about.

**Access.** Credentials, repository, platform, channels, and the escalation path. Obvious, and it is the one most often half-done.

A handover missing the second item is the common failure. People hand over operation and keep the right of veto, and three months later they are still the real owner and resent the interruptions.

## Hand over the problems too

Tell them what is wrong with it. All of it.

> The partial-settlement branch has only been exercised twice. The tolerance is two kobo because the provider rounds, and I am not certain that is right in every case. There is no eval on the summary wording. The run log has never been cleaned up and will need archiving eventually.

This feels like undermining your own work and it is the most valuable thing you can say. They will find these anyway — and finding them undisclosed teaches them that your handovers are not complete, which affects everything you give them afterwards.

## Let them change it

They will do things differently. Some will be worse, some better, and most simply different.

Unless it is genuinely dangerous — removing an idempotency key, sending without a gate — **let them**. A system its owner cannot change is not owned by them, and the alternative is that they stop changing it at all, which is how a system becomes frozen and then obsolete.

Say the boundary explicitly:

> Change anything you like. Two things I would push back on: the gate before any refund, and the idempotency key on the payout call. Both are there because of specific incidents and I will explain either one if you want to change them anyway.

That names what matters, explains that it is reasoned rather than personal, and leaves them genuinely free with everything else.

## Confirm it transferred

A week or two later, check:

- Did the alerts go to them, and did they act on them?
- Did they make a change without asking you?
- Did something break, and did they handle it?
- Are they still coming to you with questions the page should answer?

That last one is the signal. Persistent questions mean the documentation is still incomplete — fix the page, not the person.

<Callout kind="job">
In an interview, "I built it and then handed it over, and it has run for six months without me" is a much stronger sentence than "I built it".

The first describes an engineer. The second describes a dependency.
</Callout>
