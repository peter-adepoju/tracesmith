The last week. Everything built in the previous twenty-five is worth a fraction of what it could be if nobody but you can run it.

This week is handover, and it starts with documentation a stranger can follow.

## Who you are writing for

Not yourself. Not a colleague who sat next to you while you built it.

**A competent person who has never seen this, reading it under pressure, at an hour they did not choose.** Possibly after you have left.

Every decision about what to include follows from that reader.

## Open with the summary

The first paragraph answers: what is this, who is it for, what does it do, and what must the reader know before anything else.

> **Daily settlement reconciliation.** Every working morning at 08:00 WAT this compares the previous day's payouts against our transaction records and posts a summary to `#finance`, raising an alert when they disagree. It reads only — it never corrects anything. If it has not posted by 08:30, something is wrong; see *When it breaks*. Owner: Peter.

Four sentences. A reader who stops there still knows what it is, when to worry, and who to ask.

<Callout kind="key">
**Put the most important thing first, always.**

People do not read documentation; they scan it for the thing they need. A document that builds carefully to its conclusion will have the conclusion missed.

If someone reads only the first paragraph, what must they know? Put that there, and let the detail follow.
</Callout>

## The five sections

**1. What it does.** In plain language, from the business's point of view rather than the system's. "Compares payouts against transactions and reports differences", not "queries two endpoints and diffs the result sets".

**2. Where it lives.** The workflow name and link, the repository, the credential names, the tables it uses, the channels it posts to. Everything a reader must be able to find.

**3. What breaks it.** The honest list, from experience. Provider outage, expired credential, rate limit, a schema change upstream, a public holiday with no payouts. Each with what it looks like when it happens.

**4. How to fix it.** Tomorrow's lesson covers this properly. Here: the link to the runbook.

**5. Who to call.** A named owner, a named deputy, and the escalation path. "The team" is not an answer.

## Write what you were about to leave out

The things that feel too obvious while you are inside the work, and are the entire difficulty for someone outside it:

- Why it runs at 08:00 and not 07:00 — because the provider's batch lands at 07:40
- Why it reports differences instead of correcting them — because an auto-fixer that picks the wrong side makes the drift worse
- Why there is a two-kobo tolerance — because the provider rounds and we do not
- Why the credential is read-only — deliberately, and it must stay that way
- What the strange name means
- Which bit you are least confident about

That last one is the most generous thing you can write. *"The partial-settlement branch is the part I am least sure of; it has been exercised twice and both were straightforward."* It tells the next person where to look first, and it costs you nothing but the admission.

## Say what it does not do

Easily as important as what it does, and almost always missing:

> **This does not:** correct discrepancies, chase the provider, handle currencies other than NGN, cover weekend settlements (there are none), or notify anyone outside `#finance`.

Without it, someone will assume one of those is covered and plan around it. The absence is invisible until the day it matters.

## Test the page by handing it over

The only test that counts. Give it to somebody who has never seen the system and ask them to do something real — find the last run, tell you whether it succeeded, find the owner, say what would happen if the provider were down.

Watch, and do not help. Every question they ask is a gap. Every pause is ambiguity.

Then fix the document rather than answering the question, which is the discipline — answering is faster and teaches the document nothing.

<Callout kind="job">
A fifteen-minute handover test finds more than an hour of re-reading your own writing, because you cannot un-know what you know.

It is also the cheapest way to discover that the thing you thought was obvious is the thing everybody gets stuck on.
</Callout>

## Keep it near the thing

Documentation in a place nobody looks is documentation that does not exist.

- In the repository, next to the code and the prompts
- Linked from the workflow itself, in a note on the canvas
- Linked from the alert, so the person reading at 03:00 has it in front of them
- One page, not twelve — a sprawl nobody maintains is worse than a page that is slightly incomplete

And **date it**, with the owner. An undated document of unknown provenance gets treated as unreliable, usually correctly.
