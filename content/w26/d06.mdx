The last day. One real automation, built, tested, documented and handed over — meeting the full definition of done, and presented in five minutes.

Not a demo. Something that runs on Monday whether or not you are there.

## Choose it

Pick something **real and small**. A process that genuinely happens, that somebody genuinely does, that you can finish.

Good: a daily report that is currently assembled by hand. A chase that happens when someone remembers. A reconciliation between two systems. A notification that is currently somebody watching a screen.

Bad: anything you invented to have something to build. The whole value is that a real person stops doing a real thing.

If you have no work process, use your own life: a weekly summary of something you track, a reminder that depends on a real condition, a check on something you currently check by hand.

## The definition of done

Not "it works". All of it:

**Chosen and justified**
- Scored on frequency, stability and cost of error — week 20
- Payback calculated
- Written down as a one-paragraph proposal, including what you decided *not* to build

**Built**
- Trigger, call, record — week 21
- State in a table, not a document
- Logic in code where it is logic, on the canvas where it is movement

**Reliable** — week 22
- Retries with backoff on every external call
- Timeouts set deliberately, worst case inside the schedule interval
- Idempotent, proven by the run-twice test and the kill-halfway test
- An error workflow attached, alerting on two channels
- A run log you own, with items in and items processed
- A heartbeat, and something watching for its absence

**Governed** — week 25
- No invented data anywhere, including the demo
- An audit trail for anything consequential, with named actors
- Credentials in the store, scoped, with a recorded expiry
- If it uses a model: evals with a baseline, and a human gate before anything irreversible

**Documented** — week 26
- One page a stranger can follow, tested by handing it to one
- A runbook with three failure modes and the re-run safety answered
- In the repository, with the prompts and eval cases

**Measured**
- A baseline taken before
- The after measured honestly, net of maintenance
- The misses recorded

**Handed over**
- A named owner who is not you, if that is possible
- A walkthrough where they drove
- Responsibility, authority, context and access all transferred

<Callout kind="key">
**The hard parts are not the building.**

Building is the afternoon. The definition of done above is the week, and every item on it is something you have seen go wrong in the previous twenty-five.

That gap — between "it works on my machine" and "it runs on Monday whether or not I am there" — is the entire difference between the two.
</Callout>

## Break it on purpose, one more time

Before you call it done, run the week 22 day 6 drill:

1. Kill the dependency → does it retry, then alert, on both channels?
2. Break the credential → does it fail distinguishably, without retrying a 401?
3. Run it twice → does the second run change nothing?
4. Kill it halfway and re-run → does it complete the work exactly once?
5. Switch it off → does anything notice, inside the expected window?
6. Feed it bad items → does it report `partial` with real counts?

Record the gaps. Fix them. **A capstone that has never failed in front of you is not finished.**

## Present it in five minutes

The last artefact. Five minutes, in this order:

**0:00 — The problem.** One sentence, with the number. *"The daily reconciliation takes 40 minutes every morning and three discrepancies a month reach Finance uncaught."*

**0:30 — What it does now.** One sentence. *"It runs at 08:00, compares both sides, posts a summary, and alerts when they disagree. It reports; it never corrects."*

**1:00 — Show it.** The actual output in the actual channel. Thirty seconds. Not a slide of the canvas — nobody wants to look at a canvas.

**1:30 — One design decision, explained.** The one with a real trade-off. *"It reports rather than corrects, because an auto-fixer that picks the wrong side makes the drift worse and harder to find."* This is the slide that tells people whether you understand what you built.

**2:30 — Reliability.** Briefly: retries, idempotent, heartbeat, alerts on two channels, and *"I broke it six ways and here is what I found and fixed."*

**3:30 — The numbers.** Before, after, net of maintenance, and the misses.

**4:30 — Owner and what is next.** Who owns it, what you would do next, and what you deliberately did not build.

Two things to resist: showing the canvas, and explaining how clever it is. Nobody is assessing the diagram. They are assessing whether this will still be working in six months.

## Check yourself, one last time

- Does it run without me?
- Have I watched it fail, on purpose, and fixed what I found?
- Can a stranger follow my page without asking me a question?
- Have I measured the before and the after, net of maintenance?
- Does someone else own it, with the authority to change it?
- Have I told them what is wrong with it?

## The course in one line

Find out what is actually true, prove it with evidence, write it down so somebody else can act on it, and build things that keep working when you are not watching.

That is quality assurance, that is technical support, and that is automation. It was always the same job.
