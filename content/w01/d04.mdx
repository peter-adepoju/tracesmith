If you learn one thing in this phase, learn this.

Every response starts with a three-digit number. That number tells you what happened and, more usefully, **whose problem it is**. In a support queue or a bug report, it is the fastest route from "it's broken" to a specific, actionable statement.

## The five families

You do not memorise all the codes. You memorise five groups, by first digit.

| Family | Means | In plain words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1xx** | Informational | Rare. Ignore for now. |
| **2xx** | Success | It worked. |
| **3xx** | Redirect | Look somewhere else. |
| **4xx** | Client error | **You** got the request wrong. |
| **5xx** | Server error | **They** failed. |

<Callout kind="key">
The jump from 4xx to 5xx is the jump from your problem to their problem. That one boundary is the most valuable thing on this page. A 4xx means change your request. A 5xx means stop changing your request and escalate.
</Callout>

## The codes you will actually meet

**200 OK** — it worked, here is the answer.

**201 Created** — it worked and something new now exists. Usually the answer to a POST.

**204 No Content** — it worked and there is deliberately nothing to send back. Common after a DELETE.

**301 / 302 Moved** — the thing is at a different address now. Your browser follows these automatically, which is why you rarely notice them.

**400 Bad Request** — the request itself was malformed. Very often this means the body was not valid JSON, so the server could not even begin to read it.

**401 Unauthorized** — we do not know who you are. No credential, or one we do not recognise. Badly named: it is about *identity*, not permission.

**403 Forbidden** — we know exactly who you are, and you are not allowed to do this.

**404 Not Found** — there is nothing at that address. Check the path, including the version prefix.

**405 Method Not Allowed** — the address is right, the verb is wrong. Yesterday's lesson.

**422 Unprocessable Content** — the request was well-formed and parsed fine, but the content broke a rule. A missing required field, a negative amount, an invalid email.

**429 Too Many Requests** — you are going too fast. Slow down and try again later.

**500 Internal Server Error** — something broke on their side. Nothing you send will fix it.

**502 / 503 / 504** — the server is up but something behind it is not, or it is overloaded, or something timed out. Still their side.

## The four distinctions people get wrong

These four pairs come up in interviews, in tickets, and in real diagnosis. Learn them as pairs.

### 401 versus 403

**401 is identity. 403 is permission.**

A 401 means: you have not told us who you are, or the credential you sent is not one we recognise. Fix: send a valid credential.

A 403 means: your credential is fine, we know precisely who you are, and this account may not do this. Fix: get different permissions, or stop trying.

The difference matters because the actions are completely different. A 401 might be an expired token the customer can refresh themselves. A 403 is a conversation with whoever controls access.

### 404 versus 405

**404 is "no such address". 405 is "wrong verb for this address".**

A 405 is quietly good news: it proves the endpoint exists. You are closer than a 404 suggests.

### 400 versus 422

**400 usually means it did not parse. 422 means it parsed and then failed the rules.**

A 400 from a JSON API is very often a trailing comma or a missing quote. The server never got as far as your fields. A 422 means your JSON was perfect and the *content* was wrong: a field missing, a value out of range.

Knowing which you have tells you where to look: at the shape of the body, or at its values.

### 4xx versus 5xx

**4xx: change the request. 5xx: escalate.**

This is the one that saves hours. If a customer is getting a 500, nothing they type will help. Trying different inputs is wasted effort. Collect the time, the path and the request id, and send it to the people who own the server.

<Callout kind="job">
A support reply that says "this is a 500, which is a fault on our side, not something you've done wrong — I've escalated it with your request id and will update you by 4pm" is worth ten replies that ask the customer to clear their cache.
</Callout>

<RequestInspector preset="No key at all" />

Try, in this order:

1. **No key at all** → 401. You did not say who you are.
2. **Right call, wrong key** → 403. We know you; this key is not permitted here.
3. **Typo in the path** → 404. No such address.
4. **Wrong method** → 405. Right address, wrong verb.
5. **Missing a required field** → 422. Parsed fine, failed the rules.
6. **Broken JSON** → 400. Never parsed at all.
7. **Server failure** → 500. Not your problem.
