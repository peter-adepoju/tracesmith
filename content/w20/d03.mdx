Nobody can describe their own process accurately. Not because they are careless — because the awkward parts have been absorbed into habit and stopped being visible.

Mapping is how you find them before you build on top of them.

## Three columns

For each step: **what happens, who does it, which system.**

```
STEP                        WHO              SYSTEM
1  Merchant signs up        Merchant         Website
2  Welcome email sent       Automatic        Mailer
3  Documents uploaded       Merchant         Portal
4  Documents reviewed       Compliance       Portal
5  Account approved         Compliance       Admin
6  API keys issued          Automatic        Platform
7  Keys emailed             ???              ???
8  Merchant goes live       Merchant         Their site
```

Step 7 is the point of the exercise. Nobody could name the owner or the system — and in reality one person on the team does it by hand, from memory, when they notice.

<Callout kind="key">
**The step with no owner is where the process actually fails.**

It is invisible in conversation because it has always been absorbed by someone being conscientious. It becomes visible the moment they are on leave.

Mapping finds it. That alone is worth the hour, whether or not you automate anything.
</Callout>

## Draw it

Boxes and arrows beat prose, because a diagram makes gaps and loops visible in a way a numbered list does not.

```
  [Signup] → [Welcome email] → [Upload docs]
                                     ↓
                            [Compliance review]
                               ↓           ↓
                         approved       rejected
                               ↓           ↓
                       [Issue keys]   [Ask for more] ──┐
                               ↓           ↑            │
                        [Email keys]       └────────────┘
                               ↓
                           [Go live]
```

Loops are where time disappears. "Ask for more documents" going back to upload can run three times, and nobody counts it — so everyone believes onboarding takes two days when it averages nine.

## The decision nobody writes down

Every real process has at least one.

> **Step 4: compliance reviews the documents.** On what basis? What makes a document acceptable?

The documented answer is "they check it is valid". The real answer, once you ask, is a list of eleven things someone has in their head, three of which contradict the written policy, and one of which is "if it is a referral from a partner we are more lenient".

You cannot automate a decision nobody has written down. So:

1. Ask the person who makes it to talk you through five recent examples
2. Write down the rule you observe, not the rule they describe
3. Show it back and let them correct it
4. **Automate only the clear cases** and route the rest to a human

That fourth point is the whole art. A rule that handles 80% confidently and escalates the remaining 20% is far more valuable than one that handles 100% badly — and it is much easier to build.

## What to record for each step

- **Trigger** — what makes this step start?
- **Input** — what does it need?
- **Output** — what does it produce?
- **Owner** — a named person or team, or *nobody*, written honestly
- **System** — where it happens
- **Duration** — how long it takes, and how long it waits
- **Failure** — what happens when it goes wrong, and who finds out

The last two are the ones people skip, and they are where the automation case usually lives. A step taking four minutes and waiting two days is not a four-minute step.

## Wait time against work time

Map them separately. Most processes are mostly waiting:

```
Work time:  45 minutes
Wait time:  7 days
```

That tells you something important: automating the 45 minutes saves 45 minutes. **Automating the chase that removes four of the seven days is worth far more.**

New automators optimise the work and leave the waiting. The waiting is where the value is.

## Map the real process, not the official one

Ask these, and expect the answers to be uncomfortable:

- What do you do when the normal route does not work?
- What do you check that nobody asked you to check?
- What is the last thing that went wrong, and what did you do?
- Which part do you dread?
- What do you do on the days you are busy that you would not do otherwise?

That last question finds the shortcuts, and the shortcuts are the real process.
