Phase 5. Seven weeks on moving repeatable work onto systems that run themselves — and it starts, like Phase 3's automation week, with the judgement rather than the tool.

**Choosing what to automate is worth more than knowing any tool.** Tools change every eighteen months. The judgement does not.

## The three questions

**How often does it happen?** Twenty times a month is a strong case. Four times a year is not, however annoying it is each time.

**How stable is it?** Does the process work the same way it did six months ago, and will it in six more? An automation built on something actively changing breaks before it has repaid the build.

**What does a wrong result cost?** This does not decide whether to automate — it decides **how much checking to build in**. High cost of error means a human approves before anything leaves the building.

## The arithmetic

```
Hours a year saved  = (times per month × minutes each × 12) / 60
Payback in months   = build hours / hours saved per month
```

A reasonable bar: **payback within six months**, plus stability of at least 3 out of 5.

Then add the part nobody budgets for. Maintenance is real and recurring — something between 10% and 30% of the build cost, every year, forever. A thing built in 12 hours costs two to four hours a year to keep alive, and more in a year when a dependency changes under it.

<Callout kind="key">
**The time saved is not the only benefit, and often not the main one.**

**Consistency.** A person doing a 40-step reconciliation at 6pm on a Friday makes mistakes. A system does it the same way every time.

**Speed.** Something that happens within a minute instead of the next working day changes what the business can promise.

**Visibility.** An automated process leaves a record. A manual one leaves whatever someone remembered to write down.

**Attention.** The real prize. The person freed from the daily report is doing something only a person can do.
</Callout>

## Bad candidates

**Unstable.** Built on something being redesigned. You will rewrite it before it has paid for itself.

**Infrequent.** Four times a year will not repay twenty-five hours in any horizon worth planning over.

**Judgement-heavy.** If a human has to decide something difficult at every step, you are not automating the work — you are adding a layer between the person and the work.

**Already broken.** Automating a bad process gives you a bad process that runs faster, in more places, with nobody watching. Fix the process first.

**Nobody owns it.** An automation without an owner is an outage waiting for a volunteer.

<Callout kind="watch">
**Fewer working pieces beat many broken ones.**

The commonest failure in automation is not a bad build. It is twelve half-finished workflows, three of which silently stopped running in March and nobody noticed — because nobody was watching something that was supposed to be automatic.

One reliable automation with alerting is worth more than six that mostly work. Every piece you add is a piece someone has to maintain, monitor and eventually debug.

Resist the pull to build more. It is strong, because building is fun and maintaining is not.
</Callout>

## Rejecting a candidate well

Someone will ask for something that should not be built. Say no with the arithmetic:

> This happens four times a year and takes about four hours each time — so roughly sixteen hours a year. Building it is around twenty-five hours, plus maintenance, so it would not pay back for several years even if nothing changed underneath it.
>
> What I would do instead: write the steps down properly so anyone can run it, and automate the one part that is genuinely fiddly — pulling the four figures — which is about two hours of work and removes most of the error risk.
>
> If it becomes monthly, it moves straight to the top of my list.

The numbers, the alternative, and the condition that would change the answer. Exactly the structure from week 16's priority defence, because it is the same conversation.
