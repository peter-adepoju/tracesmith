Every automation starts with something making it run. Choosing that something badly is how you get a workflow that fires twice, fires at 3am in the wrong timezone, or never fires at all.

## The three kinds

**Schedule.** Runs at a fixed time. "Every weekday at 07:00."

- Use for: reports, batch jobs, reconciliation, chasing, clean-up
- Watch for: timezones, holidays, overlapping runs, and the fact that it runs whether or not there is anything to do

**Webhook.** Something else calls you when an event happens.

- Use for: reacting to a payment, a new signup, a form submission, a ticket
- Watch for: duplicates, retries, out-of-order arrival, and events you receive while already processing one
- **Always verify the signature.** Week 19's rule: an unverified webhook endpoint accepts any POST as a real event

**Manual.** A person presses a button.

- Use for: anything irreversible, anything rare, anything still being tested
- Underrated. A manual trigger with a good workflow behind it is often the right answer, and it is always the right first version

<Callout kind="key">
**Start manual, then schedule, then event-driven.**

A manual trigger lets you watch the thing run, see its output, and fix it before it has run unattended fifty times doing the wrong thing.

Promote it to a schedule once you trust it. Promote it to events once you trust it *and* latency matters.

Building straight to event-driven is how you discover a bug after it has sent three hundred emails.
</Callout>

## Polling, and why to avoid it

Checking every five minutes whether something changed. Sometimes the only option, usually the worst one:

- Wasteful — hundreds of calls to find nothing
- Slow — up to five minutes late by design
- Rate limits — you will meet them
- It needs state: *what have I already seen?* Get that wrong and you reprocess everything

If the source offers a webhook, take it. If it does not, poll on a **cursor** — the id or timestamp of the last thing you handled — never on "the last five minutes", which loses records whenever a run is slow or skipped.

## Timezones

The thing that bites hardest and is easiest to get right once.

- **Store and schedule in UTC.** Convert for display only.
- Your server, your tool and your user may be in three different zones. WAT is UTC+1 and does not observe daylight saving, which means your European integrations shift relative to you twice a year.
- "Every day at midnight" is ambiguous. Midnight where?
- "The last 24 hours" across a daylight-saving change is 23 or 25 hours.
- Month-end is not a fixed day, and a job scheduled for the 31st does not run in February.

> Schedule: `0 7 * * 1-5` — 07:00 UTC, weekdays. That is 08:00 WAT.

Write the comment. In six months nobody will remember which zone the cron was written in, and the comment is cheaper than the incident.

## A trigger that cannot fire twice

Three protections, and you want more than one:

**An idempotency key** on anything that writes. Week 13's lesson, now at the trigger: derive a key from the event — `payment:PAY-8841` — and refuse to process a key you have already handled.

**A processed-events record.** A table of event ids you have seen. Check before acting, write after succeeding. Not the other way around, or a crash mid-run loses the event.

**A lock or single-instance setting**, so a slow run does not overlap the next scheduled one. A 20-minute job on a 15-minute schedule will eventually be two copies of itself fighting over the same records.

<Callout kind="watch">
**Overlapping runs are the quietest bug in automation.**

Everything works for months. Then the data grows, a run takes longer than the interval, and two instances process the same records at once — producing duplicate emails, duplicate payments, or a corrupted state nobody can explain from the logs.

Set the single-instance option on every scheduled workflow, on the first day, before it is a problem.
</Callout>

## Retries, and what they do to you

Most sources retry. That is good for delivery and dangerous for you: your workflow must be safe to run twice on the same event, because sooner or later it will be.

The test from week 13, unchanged: **send the same event twice and count the rows.**
