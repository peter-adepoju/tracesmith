Ninety minutes, one product, five checks. This sweep is a real deliverable — it is the thing you can offer to do in a job where nobody is currently doing it, and it is a genuinely strong thing to have done when you are interviewing.

## Before you start

<Callout kind="watch">
**Written authorisation, every time.** A Slack message from your lead saying "yes, run your security checks on staging" is enough and takes ten seconds to ask for. Without it, do not start.

Then: **staging, never production.** One proof per finding. No enumeration. Nothing destructive. If you find something serious, stop testing that area and report it immediately rather than finishing the sweep first.
</Callout>

Use the Meridian lab for the practice run. Everything below is also exactly what you would do on a real product you were asked to check.

## Check 1 — access control (20 min)

Build the matrix. Every role down the side, every significant action across the top.

<AccessMatrix />

Sign in as the lowest role and send the highest actions **by API**. Expect `403`. Record every cell.

## Check 2 — tenant isolation (20 min)

For every endpoint that takes an id, send someone else's.

<IdorProbe />

Cover all six places an id hides: path, query, body, header, file download, nested child resource. Test the export endpoint first if you are short of time.

## Check 3 — sensitive data (15 min)

Open the Network panel and read the responses the product is actually sending.

<ExposureScan />

Check `/me`, any list endpoint, any search, and one error response. You are looking for credentials, full card numbers, other people's data, internal fields and infrastructure.

## Check 4 — authentication (20 min)

Copy as cURL, strip the `Authorization` header and cookies, send. Expect `401`.

Then the variations: expired token, tampered token, deactivated account, after signing out. And the hygiene checks — identical sign-in failure messages, rate limiting, cookie flags.

## Check 5 — input validation (15 min)

For every field the form validates, send the refused value directly. Then look for anything the client is trusted with — price, role, customer id, discount — and change it.

If there is an upload, test wrong type, too large, hostile name, and whether the file is readable signed out.

## The summary

This goes to someone who decides whether to release. Write it for them.

```
SECURITY SWEEP — Meridian checkout and orders
Scope       Orders, checkout, customer profile, admin orders.
            Staging only. Authorised by [name], 7 Nov.
Method      Five-check sweep, manual, API-level. One proof per
            finding; no enumeration; nothing destructive.

CRITICAL
  SEC-01  Any signed-in customer can read any order by changing
          the id (GET /api/orders/{id}). Personal data of all
          customers exposed. NDPA applies.
  SEC-02  GET /api/admin/export/customers requires no
          authentication at all.

HIGH
  SEC-03  Customer role can call GET /admin/orders and receives
          every customer's orders.
  SEC-04  GET /api/customers/me returns passwordHash and a full
          card number. Field names only in this report.

MEDIUM
  SEC-05  Support role can change product prices. Contradicts the
          stated permissions; not reachable from the interface.

NOT FOUND
  No unauthenticated write endpoints. Sign-in failure messages
  are identical. Session cookie is HttpOnly and Secure.

NOT TESTED
  Upload handling — no upload feature in scope.
  Rate limiting — needs a load tool I do not have.
  Production — out of scope by agreement.

ASSESSMENT
  Do not release. SEC-01 and SEC-02 each expose every customer's
  personal data to anyone with an account or a URL, with no
  workaround and no detection. Both are reportable breaches if
  they reach production.
```

<Callout kind="key">
**Rank by damage, not by how clever the bug is.**

An unauthenticated CSV export is boring and catastrophic. A subtle token-handling flaw is interesting and may be unreachable. The boring one goes first, every time.

And include the **Not found** section. It is the part that tells a reader what your sweep actually covered, and it is what makes the findings believable rather than a list of everything you happened to notice.
</Callout>

## Handling what you find

- **Security channel, not a public ticket.** The report is exploit instructions.
- **Immediately, not at the end of the sprint.** A critical finding is an interrupt.
- **No values, only field names.** Week 11's redaction rules at their strictest.
- **Do not fix it yourself**, and do not discuss it outside the people who need to know.
- **Do not test it on production** to see whether it is "really" there.

<Callout kind="job">
This sweep is a career move as much as a technical exercise.

Most small teams have nobody doing it. Walking into a QA role and saying "I can run a five-check security sweep on any feature, here is the format, here is one I did" puts you above a great many candidates — and the five checks are all things an ordinary tester can genuinely do.
</Callout>
