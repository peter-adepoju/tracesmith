Software does not appear in front of customers. It travels, and testing sits at a specific point on that journey.

## The environments

| Environment | What it is | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Local** | A developer's own machine | One developer |
| **Dev / integration** | Everyone's work merged together | Developers |
| **Staging / sandbox / UAT** | A full copy of production | **You** |
| **Production** | The real one, real customers, real money | Everyone |

You met this in Phase 1 as the `-dev` address rule. Here is why it exists: **each environment is a chance to find a problem before the cost of finding it goes up.**

<Callout kind="watch">
**Never run a test on production.** You have read this twice already and it is worth a third time, because the mistake is quiet and the consequences are not: a real customer charged, a real record deleted, a real email sent.

Before any action that changes something, read the address bar. Say the environment out loud if you are near production. In week 8 you built environments in Postman precisely so this is a deliberate choice rather than an oversight.
</Callout>

## Staging is not production

It is a copy, and copies differ in ways that hide bugs:

- **Less data.** A list of 50 orders behaves differently from 50,000.
- **Different data.** Test customers have tidy names. Real ones have apostrophes, accents and 60-character surnames.
- **Fewer users.** No concurrency, so race conditions never appear.
- **Different configuration.** A third party in test mode behaves differently from the real one.
- **Smaller machines.** Which hides performance problems and surfaces imaginary ones.

Knowing the differences tells you which bugs staging will **never** find, and that is worth saying in a sign-off: "tested on staging; load and concurrency behaviour is not covered by this."

## How work reaches you

Most teams run something like Scrum or Kanban. The vocabulary:

- **Backlog** — everything not yet done.
- **Sprint** — a fixed period, often two weeks, with a chosen set of work.
- **Ticket / story / issue** — one piece of work.
- **Acceptance criteria** — what must be true for it to be done. **Read these first; they are your test cases in draft.**
- **Stand-up** — a short daily sync. Say what you are testing and what is blocking you.
- **Definition of done** — the team's bar. If testing is not in it, that is worth raising.

<Callout kind="job">
The single highest-value habit here: **read the ticket before it is built, and ask your questions then.**

"What should happen if the quantity is zero?" costs five minutes before the code is written, and half a day after. A tester who asks early prevents defects rather than finding them, and that is the difference between being useful and being a bottleneck.
</Callout>

## Shift left

That habit has a name: **shifting left** — moving testing earlier on the timeline.

In practice it means reviewing acceptance criteria before development starts, asking about edge cases in planning, and writing test cases from the ticket rather than from the finished feature. Writing them from the finished feature is a trap: you end up testing what was built rather than what was asked for, and you will never notice a missing requirement.

## Your role, concretely

- **Before building:** read the ticket, ask what is ambiguous, write draft cases.
- **While building:** test on dev or a preview as soon as something exists.
- **Before release:** run your cases on staging, explore, report, retest.
- **At release:** a smoke test on production — read-only. Can you load the page, does the API answer, is the data plausible.
- **After release:** watch for what you missed. Every escaped defect is a case you did not have.
