## What QA actually is

**Testing a product to find its problems before real customers do.**

Not proving it works. That distinction is the whole discipline, and it is where most beginners go wrong: they walk the happy path, see it work, and report that the feature is fine. They have confirmed something, not tested it.

A tester's question is **"what could go wrong here?"**, asked systematically, and then answered with evidence.

## Why it matters more in some places than others

In most software a bug is annoying. In software that moves money, a bug:

- charges someone twice
- sends a settlement to the wrong account
- marks an unpaid order as paid
- exposes one customer's data to another

You have already met all four in this course, in the labs and the lessons. The cost of a missed bug is money and trust, and trust is the slower of the two to rebuild.

<Callout kind="key">
**QA and support are the same job from two directions.**

Support handles the problems customers hit. QA finds and removes those problems before customers hit them. Every bug caught in testing is a stack of tickets that never happens.

Which is why this course teaches both, and why Phase 4 will feel like a continuation rather than a change of subject.
</Callout>

## The cost curve

The earlier a defect is found, the cheaper it is. Not slightly — by orders of magnitude.

| Found | What it costs |
| --- | --- |
| While the developer is writing it | Minutes. They still remember the code |
| In code review | An hour |
| In testing | Half a day: report, fix, retest |
| In production | A day, plus support tickets, plus whatever it did while live |
| By a regulator or in the press | Weeks, and the trust does not come back |

This is why "we will test it later" is expensive, and why the most valuable thing a tester does is often not finding a bug at all — it is asking a question early enough that the bug is never written.

## You are not the enemy of the developers

Worth saying plainly, because new testers often feel adversarial, and the feeling makes them worse at the job.

You and the developers want the same thing. You report the **bug**, never the person. You describe what the system did, not who you think is to blame. When you find something, the correct tone is "here is a thing that will embarrass us if a customer finds it first", not "here is your mistake".

The practical version: **"the system did X; it should do Y"** — never "you broke X". Week 11 makes this concrete, and week 6 of Phase 5 makes it the basis of how you earn engineering's trust.

## What you will actually do

Over a week, roughly:

- Read what is being built and work out what could go wrong
- Write test cases and run them
- Explore around the edges, unscripted
- Write bug reports with evidence
- Retest fixes, and reopen the ones that are not fixed
- Say, at some point, "I do not think this is ready" — and be able to show why

The last one is the job. Everything else supports it.
