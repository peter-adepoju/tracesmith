The difference between a tester who finds things and one who does not is rarely tooling. It is where they look.

## Assume nothing is true

Every interface makes claims. A good tester treats each as a hypothesis.

| The screen says | The question |
| --- | --- |
| "Out of stock" | Can I buy it anyway? |
| "Maximum 10 characters" | What happens at 11? At 10,000? |
| "Payment successful" | Did money actually move? |
| "3 items" | Does the total match three items? |
| A disabled button | Is it disabled on the server too? |
| "Saved" | Is it there after a reload? |

Half of those you have already tested in the Meridian lab. The pattern is the same every time: **the interface tells you what it believes, and you check whether it is right.**

## The heuristics

Not rules. Prompts that generate test ideas when you are staring at a feature with no idea where to start.

**Zero, one, many.** Empty cart, one item, fifty. Empty list, one row, ten thousand.

**Boundaries.** If the maximum is 10, try 9, 10 and 11. Defects cluster at edges because that is where the comparison operators are.

**Too much.** Paste 10,000 characters. Upload a 2 GB file. Order 99,999.

**Nothing.** Submit everything blank. What is required, and is it required on the server?

**Wrong type.** Letters in a number field. A `.exe` renamed to `.pdf`.

**Negative and zero.** Especially quantities and amounts. A negative quantity producing a negative total is a money bug.

**Twice.** Click submit twice. Apply the discount twice. Send the webhook twice. You have met this three times now.

**Out of order.** Press back and resubmit. Open two tabs and act in both. Complete step 3 without step 2.

**Interrupt it.** Close the tab mid-payment. Go offline mid-upload. Lock the phone.

**Someone else's.** Change the id in the URL to another customer's.

**Awkward characters.** `O'Brien`. `Adébáyọ̀`. Emoji. A name with a space at each end.

<Callout kind="job">
Those eleven prompts are most of manual testing. Keep them somewhere you can see them. When handed a feature and no test plan, walk the list and you will have twenty cases in ten minutes — several of which nobody thought of.
</Callout>

## Follow the money, the data and the permissions

Three questions that find the serious defects:

**Where does money move?** Every amount, every total, every conversion. Check the arithmetic by hand. A money bug outranks everything.

**Where does data cross a boundary?** Between customers, between tenants, between the server and the browser. Read the raw response for what should not be there.

**Where does permission get checked?** And is it checked on the server, or only hidden in the interface?

## Deciding what to test first

You will never have time for everything. Rank by **likelihood × impact**.

Notice *admin refunds* in that list. Rarely touched, so unlikely to break — but when it does, money moves wrongly and only staff can see it, so it can run wrong for weeks before anyone notices.

**How long a defect can hide is part of its impact.** A bug customers hit immediately gets reported in an hour. A bug in a monthly batch job hides for a month.

## Staying honest

Two traps worth naming.

**Confirmation bias.** You expect it to work, so you test gently and see what you expected. The fix is to decide what you are trying to break *before* you touch it.

**Fatigue blindness.** On the fortieth run you stop seeing the screen. The fix is a written case with a specific expected result, so you are comparing rather than glancing.
