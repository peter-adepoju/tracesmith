One model step, measured properly. This is the day the week's ideas become a number you can defend.

## The task

Classify an incoming support message into **billing, technical, account** or **sales**, so it lands in the right queue.

A realistic first automation for a support team, and a good interview answer — because the hard part is not the classifier, it is knowing whether it is good enough.

## Run the evals

<EvalRunner />

Run **v1** first: 65%, thirteen of twenty. Read the seven failures before looking at v2 — they are the ones a keyword matcher always gets wrong, and each one tells you exactly what rule is missing.

Then run **v2**, which adds three rules taken from exactly those failures and nothing else. It scores 100%, and the component says the right thing about that: a perfect score on twenty cases means **write harder cases**, not that you are finished.

That loop — measure, read the failures, write one rule per failure, measure again — is the whole method.

## Reading the confusion matrix

Rows are what it should have been, columns what it predicted. The diagonal is correct; everything else is a misroute.

The pattern matters more than the total:

- **One row scattered everywhere** — that category is badly defined. Fix the definition before anything else.
- **One cell dominating off-diagonal** — two categories overlap. Add the rule that separates them.
- **A column with too much in it** — the model's default when unsure. Give it a real escape instead.

That last one is the most common. A classifier with no `unclear` option dumps its uncertainty into whichever category sounds most general.

## Held out means held out

The twenty cases must not be the examples in your prompt.

If you write a rule for *"is there a discount for annual billing?"* and then test on that sentence, you have measured nothing except your own copying. **Keep a set the prompt has never seen**, and when you use a case to fix a rule, write a new one to replace it in the set.

## Is it good enough to ship?

Accuracy alone does not answer this. Three further questions:

**1. What does a mistake cost?** A misrouted ticket goes to the wrong queue and gets moved — minutes, mildly annoying. Compare with a misrouted *refund approval*, which is money. Same accuracy, completely different decision.

**2. What is the baseline?** What happens today? If messages currently go into one undifferentiated queue, 85% accurate routing is a large improvement. If a person currently routes them at 99%, 85% is a downgrade dressed as progress.

**3. Does a mistake get caught?** A misroute a human notices within minutes is survivable at a much lower accuracy than one that disappears.

<Callout kind="key">
**Accuracy is meaningless without the cost of a mistake and the current baseline.**

"92% accurate" is not a result. "92% accurate, against a baseline of everything landing in one queue, with every misroute visible and correctable in the queue it lands in" is a result — and it is shippable at a number that would be unacceptable elsewhere.
</Callout>

## Shipping it safely

Even at a good number, do not route silently on day one:

**Stage 1 — shadow.** Classify, record the prediction, change nothing. Compare against what humans actually did for a week. This is the only measurement that uses real traffic, and it costs nothing if it is wrong.

**Stage 2 — suggest.** Show the predicted category as a default a human can change. Record how often they change it; that is your real-world accuracy.

**Stage 3 — route, with a floor.** Act on `high` confidence, send `low` and `unclear` to a human queue.

**Stage 4 — monitor.** Track the rate of manual re-categorisation. A rise means something drifted — the model, the prompt, or the kinds of messages arriving.

Never skip stage 1. A week of shadow mode on real traffic tells you more than a hundred invented test cases.

## What to record

```
CLASSIFIER — support ticket routing
Prompt       classify-ticket.v2
Model        (small), temperature 0
Eval set     20 held-out messages, none used in the prompt
Accuracy     18/20 = 90%
Failures     "payment page will not load" → billing (should be technical)
             "who can approve invoices now" → billing (should be account)
Baseline     Today: one undifferentiated queue, ~4 min to route by hand
Cost of      Low — misroute is visible and correctable in the queue
  error      it lands in
Decision     Ship in shadow mode for one week, then suggest.
             Re-evaluate before routing automatically.
Threshold    Route automatically only above 95% on a 50-case set.
```

That page is the deliverable. It states what was measured, on what, what failed, what the alternative is, and what would change the decision.

Most people demonstrating an AI feature show it working on three examples they chose.

Arriving with an eval set, an accuracy figure, the failures named, a baseline and a staged rollout plan is a completely different conversation — and it is the one that gets the thing actually turned on.
</Callout>

## Check yourself

- Were the eval cases genuinely held out of the prompt?
- Did I write one rule per observed failure, rather than rewriting the prompt wholesale?
- Do I know the baseline, or only my own number?
- What does a mistake cost, and would anyone notice?
- Am I shipping in shadow mode first?
